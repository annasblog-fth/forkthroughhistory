Arroz con Pato · Serves 4

Las Fiestas Patrias, Jul 2026 Peru Chiclayo

Arroz con Pato

The cilantro-green, beer-braised duck rice of northern Peru — fragrant, festive, and made for celebration

Serves 4  ·  Marinate overnight  ·  Braise 45–60 min

Flavor Profile

I love the herby flavors given by the cilantro and also the green visuals it provides. The rice has got a nice kick of spice and spices. What makes this dish stand apart from other dishes with various poultry served with rice, is the subtle sweetness the loche squash adds, also, giving a nice texture to the rice as well as pairing up nicely with the crisp scent from the green herbs. Every grain is flavorful after cooking in the marinade from the duck, which, the duck, after slowly braising, is nice and tender, falling apart almost. And finally, the Salsa Criolla cuts through the hearty meal, crisp and fresh.

Origin & History

Plates for a Nation's Birthday

Left: the aderezo vegetables cooking down. Right: salsa criolla — onion rinsed in cold water, then lime juice and cilantro

On the 28th of July, Peruvian streets bustle with parades and music. In the Plaza de Armas, red and white fabrics flutter and pulse in the cool wind. As the event draws out the crowd, you might find various food carts helping hungry tourists and Peruvians to anticucho, papas rellenas, and chicharrón, washed down with some Ponche de los Libertadores, the drink to toast the country’s independence. In restaurants and on family tables you might encounter flavors of Ceviche, Ají de Gallina, and Anticuchos, and the fragrant, festive, cilantro-y Arroz con Pato.

For many, Las Fiestas Patrias del Perú, or Peru’s Independence Day marks an important moment in Peruvian identity and history. Las Fiesta Patrias, as you might have noticed, is plural. Plural as in the two consecutive days, July 28 and 29, in which Peru gained its independence from Spanish conquistadors, a day for the liberation and a day for the establishment of the Republic.

Arroz con Pato is one very traditional and signature recipe representing culinary cultures of northern Peru, originating in the local farms of Chiclayo, north of Lima. The amalgamation of indigenous Moche cultures, Spanish colonial ingredients, and local herbs constructed a vibrant green rice and tender duck dish. Although this is a popular northern dish found at many celebratory events, like many historical and popular recipes, over the years it has developed into many interpretations and variations, with many families having their own recipes substituting, adding, subtracting and merging. It, in turn, continues to inspire and evolve Peruvian cuisine with its comforting presence. For example its younger sibling, Arroz con Pollo, rice with chicken.

The earliest known written reference to the dish dates back to 1860. By around 1821, near Peru's independence, the dish began appearing in accounts of French-style dinners that adopted its seasoning, at that time cooking still relied on the batán (grinding stone) to blend the ingredients that would eventually define the dish. Here are some interpretations I have found: traditional accompaniments include onion, tomato, cilantro, green peas, and sometimes corn, while many northern versions add spinach and cilantro folded into the rice, which gives it its characteristic green color. Beer is a common addition to the broth for extra depth of flavor, and some cooks blend it with a splash of pisco, while home versions from the highlands are sometimes made without beer at all.

My recipe is in no way authentic, it’s what I conjured up from the daze of browsing through many spanish translations of recipes online into something somewhat easy, tasty and accessible, so more people can explore Peru and its festivities and culture from their own kitchens.

A Note on Pairings

Arroz con Pato is a complete meal on its own, but a cold Peruvian beer — Cristal or Cusqueña, alongside it is entirely appropriate, given that beer goes into the pot anyway. A glass of chicha morada (purple corn drink) is the non-alcoholic Peruvian pairing of choice.

Ingredients

What You Need

Serves 4. Measurements are guidelines — eyeball and adjust as you go.

The Duck & Marinade

  • 1 whole duck (small), cut into 4 pieces
  • ½ bell pepper, diced
  • 70 g loche squash, grated (or butternut squash)
  • 1 bunch cilantro, roughly chopped
  • 8 tbsp ají amarillo paste (yellow chili paste)
  • 2 tbsp mirasol chili paste
  • 1 tbsp garlic paste
  • 1 tsp cumin
  • ½ tsp turmeric
  • Salt and pepper, to taste
  • 1 cup dark beer
  • ½ cup chicha de jora (optional — or sub more dark beer)

The Cilantro Aderezo (Dressing)

  • 1½ onions, roughly chopped
  • 1 ají amarillo (yellow chili pepper)
  • ½ bell pepper
  • A small handful of loche squash
  • 1 small bunch parsley
  • 1 small bunch cilantro
  • ½ tbsp garlic paste
  • 1 cup duck or chicken stock
  • Salt, cumin, and pepper, to taste

The Rice

  • 1 kg rice, rinsed clean
  • Blended cilantro aderezo (from above)
  • 1 cup green peas
  • Duck broth (from braising)
  • Salt, to taste

Salsa Criolla (side)

  • 1 red onion, very thinly sliced
  • 1 ají limo (or any hot chili), thinly sliced
  • A handful of cilantro, roughly chopped
  • Salt, to taste
  • Lime juice, to taste
Method

Steps

The Night Before — Marinate the Duck

  1. Dice all your vegetables and herbs. In a large bowl, combine the duck pieces with the diced bell pepper, grated loche squash, and chopped cilantro. Add the mirasol chili paste, ají amarillo paste, garlic paste, cumin, turmeric, salt, and pepper. Pour in the chicha de jora and dark beer. Mix thoroughly until everything is well coated. Cover and refrigerate overnight.

The Next Day — Make the Cilantro Aderezo

  1. Roughly chop the onion, ají amarillo, bell pepper, and loche squash.
  2. Heat a splash of oil in a pan over medium heat. Add the chopped vegetables and sauté for 8–10 minutes, seasoning with salt, cumin, and pepper as they cook down and soften.
  3. Stir in the roughly torn cilantro, parsley, and garlic paste. Cook for 2 more minutes, then turn off the heat and let cool slightly.
  4. Transfer the mixture to a blender with the stock and blend until smooth and vibrantly green. Set aside.

Cook the Duck

  1. Remove the duck from the marinade (keep all the marinade). Heat a splash of oil in a heavy pan or Dutch oven and sear the duck pieces for 4–5 minutes per side until nicely browned.
  2. Transfer the seared duck to a pot and pour in the reserved marinade. Cover and braise over medium-low heat for 45 minutes to 1 hour, until the duck is fully cooked and tender enough to pull.
  3. Remove the duck and set it aside in a separate container with a ladleful of the braising liquid to keep it moist. Reserve the rest of the braising broth to cook the rice.

Cook the Rice

  1. In a wide pot, add the blended green aderezo. Stir in the rinsed rice and peas. Taste the aderezo and adjust the salt — remember the broth you'll be adding is savory, so season lightly now.
  2. Add enough duck broth to just cover the rice. Cook over medium-high heat, stirring constantly so the rice doesn't stick to the bottom. Once the liquid has dropped to just level with the rice, lower the heat to minimum, cover tightly, and cook for about 12 minutes until the rice is moist and tender. Tip: don't add too much liquid at once — the rice becomes porridge very fast. Ask me how I know.

Make the Salsa Criolla (While the Rice Cooks)

  1. Combine the thinly sliced red onion, chopped cilantro, and sliced chili in a bowl. Give the onion slices a quick rinse under cold water first to take the edge off their sharpness. Season with salt and lime juice. Set aside.

To Serve

  1. Spoon a generous mound of the steaming green rice onto the plate. Lay a piece of braised duck alongside. Finish with the salsa criolla piled on top — its brightness is not optional. Enjoy all that hard work.
Notes & Tips

Loche squash: If you can't find loche, butternut squash is the best substitute. Kabocha works too. Grate it directly into the marinade and aderezo.

Chicha de jora: This fermented corn beer adds a subtle tang and depth. If unavailable, just increase the dark beer. A splash of apple cider vinegar can approximate the fermented note.

Ají amarillo paste: Found in most Latin American grocery stores or online. The flavor is fruity and distinctively Peruvian — don't substitute with generic chili paste if you can help it.

Beer in the braise: Use a dark beer with some body — a stout or porter works beautifully. The bitterness mellows in the braise and adds real depth.

Rice ratio: This is the trickiest part. Go conservative with the broth — you can always add more, but you can't take it back. The rice should finish moist and green, not soupy.

Storage

How Long It Keeps

Refrigerator

Store leftovers in an airtight container and refrigerate immediately — do not let the rice sit at room temperature for long. Best eaten within 3 days.

To refresh: stir in a few spoonfuls of water before warming to bring the rice back to life. The duck reheats well with its reserved braising liquid.

Plated — green rice, braised duck, corn, and salsa criolla

Arroz con Pato is a dish that rewards patience. The overnight marinade, the slow braise, the careful ratio of broth to rice — it asks you to slow down, and in return it gives you something genuinely extraordinary. For me, cooking dishes like this is how I get closest to understanding a culture from the outside: standing at the stove, translating someone else's recipe, trying to understand what they loved about it.

¡Felices Fiestas Patrias!
